# PW-027: SYSVOL permissions differ from the GPO's AD permissions

**Category:** Hygiene
**Typical owner:** AD / identity team

## What this finding means
A Group Policy Object has two halves: the object in Active Directory
(`CN={GUID},CN=Policies,CN=System,...`) and a folder in SYSVOL
(`\\<domain>\SYSVOL\<domain>\Policies\{GUID}`) that holds its settings,
scripts and preference files. Group Policy Management keeps the folder's
permissions in step with the object's when you delegate through it.

Here the folder (or its `Machine`, `User` or `Scripts` subfolder) lets an
account or group write files although it has no right to edit the GPO in
AD. Default writers are not reported: SYSTEM, CREATOR OWNER, BUILTIN
Administrators, Enterprise Domain Controllers, Domain Admins, Enterprise
Admins and Group Policy Creator Owners.

Pathwarden also draws this as a `WriteGPOFiles` relationship from the account or group to the
GPO. When the GPO applies to Tier Zero (or to a Privilege Zone), that relationship is
an attack path and PW-003 (or PW-022) reports it as well.

## Why attackers care
Whoever writes the folder decides what the GPO does: a startup script in
`Machine\Scripts`, a scheduled task in `Preferences\ScheduledTasks`, a new
local administrator in `Groups.xml`, a user right in `GptTmpl.inf`
(Domain or Tenant Policy Modification: Group Policy Modification, T1484.001).
Every computer and user the GPO is linked to runs it at the next refresh. The
Delegation tab in Group Policy Management doesn't show folder permissions, so
this access is easy to miss.

## Before you change anything
- Find out why the permission is there: a deployment tool, an old migration
  (SYSVOL copied by hand, FRS to DFSR), a script that sets ACLs, or a
  helpdesk shortcut.
- If the account or group really needs to edit the GPO, delegate it properly in
  Group Policy Management (Delegation tab, **Edit settings**), which sets both
  halves.
- Save the folder's current permissions so you can roll back:
  `icacls "\\<domain>\SYSVOL\<domain>\Policies\{GUID}" /save gpo-acl.txt /t`

## Fix
### Option A: Let Group Policy Management make them consistent (recommended)
1. Open **Group Policy Management** as a Domain Admin and select the GPO.
2. When the folder's permissions differ from the object's, it warns that the
   SYSVOL permissions are inconsistent and offers to make them match. Click
   **OK**. This rewrites the SYSVOL folder permissions from the GPO's Group
   Policy security permissions, so Authenticated Users keeps its read access
   if the GPO grants it.
3. Check the **Delegation** tab: only the accounts and groups that should edit the GPO
   are listed.
### Option B: Remove the permission with icacls
The finding's fix lists each folder and the command, for example:
```powershell
icacls "\\scale.local\SYSVOL\scale.local\Policies\{GUID}" /remove:g *S-1-5-21-...-1105
```
`/remove:g` removes every allow entry of the account or group on that folder,
read access included. For Authenticated Users, Domain Computers or another broad
group, give Read & Execute back straight after, or the GPO can stop applying
(Group Policy reads the folder to apply it):
```powershell
icacls "<folder>" /remove:g *S-1-5-11
icacls "<folder>" /grant *S-1-5-11:(OI)(CI)RX
```
An entry marked `(I)` in `icacls` output is inherited: remove it on the parent
folder instead. For a folder the account or group owns, set the owner back:
`icacls "<folder>" /setowner *S-1-5-32-544`.

If nobody uses the GPO any more, unlink and delete it instead.

Also look at the files inside for anything the account or group may have planted
(scripts, `ScheduledTasks.xml`, `Groups.xml`, `GptTmpl.inf`), and compare the
GPO's settings report with what you expect.

## Verify
`icacls "<folder>"` no longer lists the account or group. Collect again with the
built-in collector (on Windows, as a domain user) and confirm the finding ID
is gone.

## Rollback
`icacls "\\<domain>\SYSVOL\<domain>\Policies" /restore gpo-acl.txt` puts back
the permissions saved before the change (run it on the parent folder of the
one you saved).
