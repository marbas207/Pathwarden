All definitions
Tier Zero
The accounts, groups and servers that control the whole domain: domain controllers, Domain Admins and similar groups, the AD CS servers, and the GPOs and containers above them. Whoever controls one of them controls everything.
Status
Critical: a group that holds nearly every account (Domain Users, Authenticated Users, Everyone) can reach Tier Zero, or a critical finding is open. High: at least one account outside Tier Zero can reach it. Moderate: no account can, but findings at medium or above are open. Low: no account can, and nothing at medium or above is open. Any path into Tier Zero is a way to take over the domain, so the status is never Low while one is open, however small a share of the domain it is. The line under it says why, as what an attacker can do.
Accounts with an attack path to Tier Zero
Enabled users and computers outside Tier Zero with a full path to it. Each is a way in: an attacker who takes over any one of them can take over the domain.
Share of active accounts
The same accounts as a share of the active users and computers (enabled, outside Tier Zero, logged on in the last 90 days before the collection, or with a path). Accounts nobody has used in that time count only if they have a path, so old objects can't dilute it. It shows how widely exposure is spread; on a large domain it stays small even with hundreds of ways in, so it doesn't set the status.
Choke points
The points many attack paths pass through: fixing these closes every path to Tier Zero, the one that cuts the most first (the remediation path). They cut paths; they do not fix weak accounts or hygiene on its own.
Open findings
Findings not fixed or accepted as a risk. Attack path findings are on a path to Tier Zero and are rated by how many users and computers can use them; hygiene findings are weaknesses that are not, yet.
Tier Zero protected (Protection Score)
The share of Tier Zero objects that no open attack path finding leads to. Higher is better.
Collection
The share of active computers whose sessions and local groups were collected. Paths through logged-on sessions and local administrator rights are only as complete as these numbers; below 80% for a domain, the counts can read low.
Tiers
Tier Zero and each Privilege Zone in the settings: its status, the accounts outside it (and outside Tier Zero) with an attack path to it, and its Protection Score. With a run history, a status that changed says what it was the run before. Choke points are worked out for Tier Zero only; a zone shows its count.
Since the last run
The change from the previous run of the same environment in the run history, when there is one, compared as pathwarden history compares them; "What changed" lists it line by line. Besides the counts, it names what they don't show: older findings that gained or lost objects, new members of Tier Zero and the zones, and paths into them that appeared or went. Counts measured another way (an older exposure basis) are not compared.
Introduced
Net new over a period: a finding first seen in it (New), a finding fixed before and back in it (Back), an older finding that gained objects in it (+N objects), a new member of Tier Zero or a zone, or a new path into one. The Findings tab filters by it; pathwarden changes --since and pathwarden findings --introduced list the same.
First seen
When Pathwarden first found a finding, an object in it, a member or a path: the date of that run ("first seen 18 Oct (run 3)"), not when the change was made in the directory. Objects, members and paths are dated from the first run that began dating them; one that left and returned is "back".
New paths and members
A new member: an object that joined Tier Zero or a Privilege Zone, such as a user added to Domain Admins. A new path: an edge into Tier Zero or a zone from outside it (a permission, an admin right, a session) that wasn't there at the run before. CriticalPW-006Kerberoastable account with Tier Zero access10 findings
How to fix this kind of findingstep-by-step guide

Category: Attack path Typical owner: AD / identity team with the service owner

What this finding means

A user account with a service principal name (SPN) is in Tier Zero or has a path to it. Any domain user can request a Kerberos service ticket for it, and that ticket is encrypted with a key derived from the account's password.

Group and standalone managed service accounts (gMSA, sMSA) are not reported: the domain gives them a 240-byte (120-character) random password and rotates it, so their tickets can't be cracked. In SharpHound collections Pathwarden recognises them from the gmsa and msa flags; in RustHound-CE and built-in collections, which have no such flag, from the computer-account bit (userAccountControl 0x1000) on a user object. In those collections a user account an administrator gave the computer-account bit by hand, which still has a human-set password, is treated as managed and not reported: check SPN accounts with that bit (Get-ADUser -LDAPFilter "(userAccountControl:1.2.840.113556.1.4.803:=4096)") if you collect with RustHound-CE or the built-in collector.

Why attackers care

Kerberoasting (Steal or Forge Kerberos Tickets: Kerberoasting, T1558.003) needs only a normal domain account: request tickets for every SPN, take them offline and crack the passwords. Service account passwords are often old, human-chosen and never rotated, and RC4 tickets crack quickly. A cracked privileged service account means domain compromise without any alert on the target.

Before you change anything

  • Identify the service using the SPN (setspn -L <Account>), the hosts running it, and who owns it.
  • Check whether the SPN is still used: event 4769 on domain controllers shows service ticket requests for the account. No requests over weeks often means a stale SPN.
  • If the account is privileged only because of a group it does not need (common for old SQL service accounts in Domain Admins), removing that membership is part of the fix.

Fix

Option A: Move the service to a group Managed Service Account (recommended)
  1. Create a gMSA (New-ADServiceAccount) allowed for the service's hosts.
  2. Install it on the hosts (Install-ADServiceAccount) and change the service to run as <Domain>\<gMSA>$ with an empty password.
  3. Move the SPNs to the gMSA, then disable the old account. gMSA passwords are 240-byte (120-character) random values rotated automatically: not crackable.
Option B: Remove unneeded SPNs or privileges

Remove stale SPNs with setspn -D, and remove the account from privileged groups it does not need.

Option C: If the account must stay

Set a random password of 25+ characters, enable AES-only Kerberos for the account ("This account supports Kerberos AES 128/256 bit encryption" in ADUC Account tab), and rotate the password on a schedule.

PowerShell
Import-Module ActiveDirectory

# Kerberoastable user accounts
Get-ADUser -Filter { ServicePrincipalName -like "*" } -Properties ServicePrincipalName, PasswordLastSet, MemberOf

# Option A: create a gMSA (needs a KDS root key in the forest)
New-ADServiceAccount -Name "<gMSA>" -DNSHostName "<gMSA>.<Domain>" `
    -PrincipalsAllowedToRetrieveManagedPassword "<ServerGroup>"
Install-ADServiceAccount -Identity "<gMSA>"     # run on each service host

# Option B: remove a stale SPN
setspn -D <SPN> <Account>

# Option C: AES only (0x18 = AES128 + AES256), then reset the password
Set-ADUser -Identity "<Account>" -Replace @{ 'msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes' = 24 }
Set-ADAccountPassword -Identity "<Account>" -Reset -NewPassword (Read-Host -AsSecureString "New password")

Verify

Get-ADUser "<Account>" -Properties ServicePrincipalName shows no SPNs (Options A/B), or msDS-SupportedEncryptionTypes is 24 and PasswordLastSet is recent (Option C). Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone.

Rollback

Re-register an SPN with setspn -S <SPN> <Account>; switch the service back to the old account in the Services console. Keep the old account disabled, not deleted, until the gMSA has run cleanly for a while.

HighPW-004Tier Zero account logged on to non-Tier Zero hosts4 findings · 8 objects
How to fix this kind of findingstep-by-step guide

Category: Attack path Typical owner: AD / identity team together with the admins who own the account

What this finding means

A Tier Zero account (for example a Domain Admin) had a session on servers or workstations outside Tier Zero. While logged on, and often afterwards, its credentials are in that host's memory, so anyone with admin rights on the host can take them.

Why attackers care

Attackers who gain local admin on any listed host dump LSASS or steal Kerberos tickets (OS Credential Dumping: LSASS Memory, T1003.001) and immediately become Domain Admin. This is one of the most common paths in real intrusions and pentests, and usually the biggest choke point in pathwarden plan.

Before you change anything

  • Ask the account owner why they log on to those hosts (RDP, scheduled task, service, mapped drive). Event 4624 on the host shows logon type: 2/10 interactive or RDP, 3 network, 4 batch, 5 service.
  • Services or scheduled tasks running as a Tier Zero account must be moved to a dedicated, non-privileged service account (or gMSA) first, or they will break.
  • Make sure each admin has a separate, non-Tier Zero account for daily work.

Fix

Option A: Separate accounts per tier and deny Tier Zero logon elsewhere (recommended)
  1. Give the admin a normal account for workstations and a Tier 1 account for servers if needed.
  2. In Group Policy Management, edit a GPO linked to all non-Tier Zero computers: Computer Configuration > Policies > Windows Settings > Security Settings > Local Policies > User Rights Assignment.
  3. Add the Tier Zero groups (Domain Admins, Enterprise Admins, Schema Admins) to Deny log on locally, Deny log on through Remote Desktop Services, Deny log on as a batch job and Deny log on as a service.
  4. Log off the account on the listed hosts, or reboot them, to clear cached credentials.
Option B: Authentication Policy Silos
  1. In Active Directory Administrative Center > Authentication, create an authentication policy and silo that allows Tier Zero accounts to authenticate only from Tier Zero hosts (requires Windows Server 2012 R2+ domain functional level).
  2. Assign the accounts and Tier Zero hosts to the silo; test in audit mode first.
Option C: Protected Users

Add the account to Protected Users: no NTLM, no delegation, no cached credentials, 4-hour TGTs. Test first: it breaks NTLM-only apps for that account.

PowerShell
Import-Module ActiveDirectory

# Where has the account logged on recently? (run on a host from the finding)
Get-WinEvent -FilterHashtable @{ LogName = 'Security'; Id = 4624 } -MaxEvents 2000 |
    Where-Object { $_.Properties[5].Value -eq '<Account>' } |
    Select-Object TimeCreated, @{ n = 'LogonType'; e = { $_.Properties[8].Value } }

# Services and scheduled tasks running as the account on a host
Get-CimInstance Win32_Service -ComputerName <Computer> | Where-Object StartName -like '*<Account>*'
Get-ScheduledTask -CimSession <Computer> | Where-Object { $_.Principal.UserId -like '*<Account>*' }

# Add the account to Protected Users (after testing)
Add-ADGroupMember -Identity "Protected Users" -Members "<Account>"

Verify

After the deny rights apply (gpupdate /force, or the next refresh), a logon attempt by the Tier Zero account on a listed host must fail. Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone.

Rollback

Remove the groups from the Deny user rights in the GPO (or unlink it) and run gpupdate /force; remove the account from Protected Users with Remove-ADGroupMember "Protected Users" -Members "<Account>".

HighPW-002Non-Tier Zero principal can DCSync1 finding · 2 objects
How to fix this kind of findingstep-by-step guide

Category: Attack path Typical owner: AD / identity team

What this finding means

A user, computer or group outside Tier Zero holds both directory replication rights on the domain object: "Replicating Directory Changes" and "Replicating Directory Changes All" (directly or through group membership). Together they let it ask a domain controller for the password hash of any account.

Why attackers care

DCSync (OS Credential Dumping: DCSync, T1003.006) lets an attacker who controls this principal pull the krbtgt hash and every admin hash remotely, without touching a domain controller's disk. With the krbtgt hash they can forge golden tickets and keep domain access until krbtgt is rotated twice. Tools such as Mimikatz and Impacket secretsdump do this in one command.

Before you change anything

  • Identify what the principal is. Legitimate holders besides domain controllers are rare: Entra Connect (MSOL_ / AAD sync) accounts, some identity governance, backup or password-sync products.
  • Check whether it actually replicates: on domain controllers, audit "Directory Service Access" and look for event 4662 with the replication GUIDs 1131f6aa-9c07-11d1-f79f-00c04fc2dcd2 and 1131f6ad-9c07-11d1-f79f-00c04fc2dcd2 and the principal as subject.
  • If it is a sync account that must keep the rights, do not remove them: treat the account and its server as Tier Zero instead (Option B).

Fix

Option A: Remove the replication rights (recommended when not needed)
  1. In Active Directory Users and Computers, enable View > Advanced Features.
  2. Right-click the domain, Properties > Security > Advanced.
  3. Find entries for the principal (or the group that grants it) with "Replicating Directory Changes" / "Replicating Directory Changes All".
  4. Select each and click Remove, then OK.
  5. If the right comes through a group, remove the principal from that group instead.
Option B: Keep the rights, manage the principal as Tier Zero
  1. Add the account and the server it runs on to tier_zero in pathwarden.toml.
  2. Restrict logon to that server to Tier Zero admins, protect the account (long random password or gMSA where the product supports it), and monitor event 4662 for replication from any other source.
PowerShell
Import-Module ActiveDirectory
$domainDN = (Get-ADDomain).DistinguishedName
$acl = Get-Acl -Path "AD:\$domainDN"

# Replication extended rights
$getChanges    = [Guid]"1131f6aa-9c07-11d1-f79f-00c04fc2dcd2"
$getChangesAll = [Guid]"1131f6ad-9c07-11d1-f79f-00c04fc2dcd2"

# Show who holds them
$acl.Access | Where-Object { $_.ObjectType -in $getChanges, $getChangesAll } |
    Select-Object IdentityReference, ObjectType, IsInherited

# Save the current descriptor, then remove the principal's entries
$acl.Sddl | Out-File ".\domain-acl-backup.txt"
$acl.Access | Where-Object {
    $_.ObjectType -in $getChanges, $getChangesAll -and $_.IdentityReference -like "*<Account>"
} | ForEach-Object { [void]$acl.RemoveAccessRule($_) }
Set-Acl -Path "AD:\$domainDN" -AclObject $acl

Verify

Re-run the "Show who holds them" query; only domain controllers, Enterprise Domain Controllers, Administrators and intended sync accounts should remain. Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone.

Rollback

Restore the saved descriptor: $acl = Get-Acl "AD:\$domainDN"; $acl.SetSecurityDescriptorSddlForm((Get-Content .\domain-acl-backup.txt)); Set-Acl "AD:\$domainDN" $acl

How to fix this kind of findingstep-by-step guide

Category: Hygiene Typical owner: AD / identity team

What this finding means

These Tier Zero user accounts are neither members of the Protected Users group nor marked "Account is sensitive and cannot be delegated". Their credentials can therefore be cached on the machines they log on to, and their Kerberos tickets can be forwarded by any service trusted for delegation.

Why attackers care

An attacker who controls a server trusted for unconstrained or constrained delegation can impersonate any delegatable account that authenticates to it, including Domain Admins (Kerberos delegation abuse, MITRE ATT&CK T1558). Accounts outside Protected Users also leave NTLM hashes and long-lived credentials in LSASS memory, which tools like Mimikatz extract (OS Credential Dumping, T1003.001). Protected Users removes both of these options for its members.

Before you change anything

  • Protected Users members cannot use NTLM, DES or RC4 Kerberos encryption, Kerberos delegation, or cached (offline) logons, and their Kerberos TGTs last 4 hours. Anything that relies on these will break for the account.
  • Never add service accounts or computer accounts to Protected Users. Use the "sensitive and cannot be delegated" flag (Option B) for service accounts that are Tier Zero.
  • Check for NTLM use by the account first: enable NTLM auditing ("Network security: Restrict NTLM: Audit NTLM authentication in this domain") and review event 8004 on domain controllers, or 4624 events with Authentication Package NTLM.
  • Make sure the domain functional level is Windows Server 2012 R2 or later. Protected Users only takes full effect from that level.
  • Keep at least one break-glass account outside Protected Users in case Kerberos fails. Store its password offline.

Fix

Option A: Add Tier Zero user accounts to Protected Users (recommended)
  1. Open Active Directory Users and Computers (dsa.msc).
  2. Go to the Users container and open the Protected Users group.
  3. Members tab > Add. Add one test admin account first.
  4. Sign out and sign back in with that account. Confirm your admin tools still work.
  5. Add the remaining interactive Tier Zero admin accounts listed in the finding.
Option B: Mark the account "sensitive and cannot be delegated"

Use this for accounts that cannot join Protected Users, such as Tier Zero service accounts.

  1. In dsa.msc, open the account > Account tab.
  2. Under Account options, tick Account is sensitive and cannot be delegated.
  3. Click OK.
PowerShell
Import-Module ActiveDirectory

# Option A: add an interactive admin account to Protected Users
Add-ADGroupMember -Identity "Protected Users" -Members "<Account>"

# Option B: block delegation for an account that cannot join Protected Users
Set-ADUser -Identity "<Account>" -AccountNotDelegated $true

Verify

# Members of Protected Users
Get-ADGroupMember -Identity "Protected Users" | Select-Object SamAccountName

# Accounts flagged as not delegatable
Get-ADUser -Identity "<Account>" -Properties AccountNotDelegated |
    Select-Object SamAccountName, AccountNotDelegated

Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone (or lists fewer accounts).

Rollback

  • Remove the account from the group: Remove-ADGroupMember -Identity "Protected Users" -Members "<Account>". The change applies at the account's next logon. Existing tickets expire within 4 hours.
  • Clear the flag: Set-ADUser -Identity "<Account>" -AccountNotDelegated $false.

What this finding means

Why attackers care

Dormant privileged accounts are ideal targets. Their passwords are often old or reused, and nobody notices when they are used again. Before you change anything

  • Confirm with the account owner (from the description, the manager attribute or your IAM system) that the account is not needed.

  • For break-glass accounts that are meant to be unused, document them and suppress the finding in pathwarden.toml with an expiry date instead of disabling them.

Fix

Option A: Disable the account (recommended)
  1. Open Active Directory Users and Computers (dsa.msc).
  2. Find the account, right-click > Disable Account.

If the account must stay enabled, for example for a rarely used process:

  1. Open the account > Member Of tab.

  3. Grant only the specific rights the process needs.

    Select-Object SamAccountName, Enabled, MemberOf

Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone (or lists fewer accounts).

Rollback

  • Re-enable the account with Enable-ADAccount -Identity "<Account>".

How to fix this kind of findingstep-by-step guide

Category: Hygiene Typical owner: AD / identity team (service account owners for service accounts)

What this finding means

These Tier Zero accounts have passwords older than the configured limit (365 days by default). The longer a privileged password lives, the more places it may have leaked to: old backups, scripts, memory dumps, password managers of former staff, or earlier breaches.

Why attackers care

Attackers routinely recover old credential material, such as NTDS.dit backups, LSASS dumps from decommissioned servers and cracked hashes from earlier intrusions. A password that has not changed since then still works (Valid Accounts: Domain Accounts, MITRE ATT&CK T1078.002). If an attacker has the NTLM hash of an unchanged account, pass-the-hash keeps working until the password changes (Use Alternate Authentication Material: Pass the Hash, T1550.002).

Before you change anything

  • For service accounts, find every place the password is configured (services, scheduled tasks, IIS application pools, application config files, scripts) before rotating. Check 4625 failures on domain controllers right after the change to catch anything you missed.
  • Check for "password never expires": Get-ADUser <Account> -Properties PasswordNeverExpires.
  • Interactive admins should change their own password so they choose and store it securely.
  • Prefer migrating service accounts to group Managed Service Accounts (gMSA), which rotate automatically.

Fix

Option A: Rotate the password now (recommended)
  1. Open Active Directory Users and Computers (dsa.msc).
  2. Right-click the account > Reset Password.
  3. Set a long random password (25+ characters for service accounts) and store it in your privileged access vault.
  4. For service accounts, update every place that uses the password, then restart the dependent services.
Option B: Move service accounts to a gMSA
  1. Create a gMSA (New-ADServiceAccount) that the hosts running the service are allowed to use.
  2. Install it on those hosts (Install-ADServiceAccount) and reconfigure the service to run as <Domain>\<gMSA>$ with an empty password.
  3. Disable the old account once the service runs under the gMSA.
PowerShell
Import-Module ActiveDirectory

# Review password age
Get-ADUser -Identity "<Account>" -Properties PasswordLastSet, PasswordNeverExpires |
    Select-Object SamAccountName, PasswordLastSet, PasswordNeverExpires

# Reset the password (prompts for the new value without echoing it)
Set-ADAccountPassword -Identity "<Account>" -Reset -NewPassword (Read-Host -AsSecureString "New password")

# Optional: let the normal password policy apply again
Set-ADUser -Identity "<Account>" -PasswordNeverExpires $false

Verify

Get-ADUser -Identity "<Account>" -Properties PasswordLastSet |
    Select-Object SamAccountName, PasswordLastSet

Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone (or lists fewer accounts).

Rollback

A password cannot be "un-rotated". If a service breaks, set the new password wherever the service is configured (or reset again to a value you control and update the service), then restart the service. Check 4625 events to find the failing host.

MediumPW-011Computers without LAPS1 finding · 538 computers
How to fix this kind of findingstep-by-step guide

Category: Hygiene Typical owner: Desktop / server team, with the AD team for schema and permissions

What this finding means

These enabled computers (domain controllers excluded) do not have their local administrator password managed by LAPS. Their built-in local Administrator password is probably set by an image or a script, and is often identical across many machines.

Standalone managed service accounts (sMSA) are not reported: they appear as computer-like objects but have no interactive local administrator, so LAPS doesn't apply. Pathwarden recognises them from SharpHound's msa flag, and in RustHound-CE or built-in collections (which type an sMSA as a computer with no flag) from its membership of the domain's Managed Service Accounts container.

Why attackers care

After compromising one computer, an attacker dumps the local Administrator hash from the SAM and tries it everywhere. If the password is shared, every computer with the same password falls (Use Alternate Authentication Material: Pass the Hash, MITRE ATT&CK T1550.002, and Lateral Movement via SMB/Windows Admin Shares, T1021.002). This is one of the most common ways a single phished workstation turns into domain-wide compromise during a pentest.

Before you change anything

  • Windows LAPS is built into Windows 10/11 and Windows Server 2019 and later with the April 2023 update. Older systems need legacy Microsoft LAPS, or an upgrade.
  • Decide who may read passwords. By default only Domain Admins can. Grant read access to a dedicated helpdesk/desktop group per OU, never to broad groups.
  • Check for tooling that relies on the shared local admin password (deployment scripts, remote support tools) and move it to LAPS retrieval or domain accounts first.
  • The schema update is forest-wide and needs Schema Admins rights. Plan it with the AD team.

Fix

Option A: Deploy Windows LAPS by Group Policy (recommended)
  1. Extend the schema once: run Update-LapsADSchema as a Schema Admin.
  2. Let computers write their own password: Set-LapsADComputerSelfPermission -Identity "<OU>".
  3. Grant read access to the support group: Set-LapsADReadPasswordPermission -Identity "<OU>" -AllowedPrincipals "<Domain>\<SupportGroup>".
  4. In Group Policy Management, create a GPO linked to the OU and open Computer Configuration > Policies > Administrative Templates > System > LAPS.
  5. Enable Configure password backup directory = Active Directory, and set Password Settings (complexity, length 15+, age 30 days).
  6. Run gpupdate /force on a test computer and check that a password appears (see Verify).
  7. Widen the GPO to the remaining OUs in the finding.
Option B: Intune (for Entra-joined or co-managed devices)

Use an Intune Endpoint security > Account protection > Local admin password solution policy with the backup directory set to Active Directory (for hybrid-joined) or Entra ID.

PowerShell
# One-time forest preparation (Schema Admins)
Update-LapsADSchema

# Per OU: allow computers to update their own password attributes
Set-LapsADComputerSelfPermission -Identity "<OU>"

# Per OU: allow the support group to read passwords
Set-LapsADReadPasswordPermission -Identity "<OU>" -AllowedPrincipals "<Domain>\<SupportGroup>"

# After GPO deployment: confirm a computer has a managed password
Get-LapsADPassword -Identity "<Computer>" -AsPlainText

Verify

Get-LapsADPassword -Identity "<Computer>" returns a password and an expiration time. On the client, the event log Applications and Services Logs > Microsoft > Windows > LAPS > Operational shows successful password updates. Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone (or lists fewer computers).

Rollback

Unlink or disable the LAPS GPO. Computers keep their last LAPS-set password, so retrieve it with Get-LapsADPassword before removing read permissions. The schema extension is permanent but harmless.

What this finding means

A principal that is neither in the named Privilege Zone nor in Tier Zero has a right over an object inside the zone. That could be an AD permission (GenericAll, WriteDacl, WriteOwner, ForceChangePassword, AddMember, ...), local administrator or remote access rights on a zone computer, delegation rights, or a GPO link. Whoever controls that principal can take over the zone object, so the zone boundary your tiering model assumes does not hold.

Why attackers care

Attackers rarely go for the target directly. They compromise whatever is easiest (a helpdesk account, an old service account, a workstation) and follow permissions from there. A single leftover delegation, for example a support group with local admin rights on a sensitive server, turns any compromise of that support group into a compromise of the zone (Account Manipulation, MITRE ATT&CK T1098, and Remote Services, T1021). The finding's exposure figure shows how many users and computers can reach the controlling principal.

Before you change anything

  • Decide whether the principal should administer the zone. If it should, the right answer is to add it to the zone definition in pathwarden.toml (and protect it like the zone). Do not remove the permission.
  • Find what uses the right: for local admin rights, check 4624 logon events (type 3/10) on the computer. For AD permissions, check the account's role with its owner.
  • If the finding says the permission is inherited, change it on the parent OU or container. That change affects every object below it, so review those objects too.

Fix

Option A: Remove the right (recommended)

The finding's "fix" line names the exact relationship. Common cases:

  1. AD permission (ACE): In Active Directory Users and Computers, enable View > Advanced Features, open the object (or the parent OU for inherited ACEs) > Security > Advanced, select the entry for the principal and click Remove.
  2. Local Administrators / Remote Desktop Users: remove the principal from the local group on the computer, or from the GPO / Intune policy that adds it (Computer Configuration > Preferences > Control Panel Settings > Local Users and Groups, or Restricted Groups).
  3. Group membership (AddMember on a zone group): remove the permission from the group's Security tab as in step 1.
  4. GPO link / GPO edit rights: restrict who can edit the linked GPO (Group Policy Management > GPO > Delegation), or unlink it from the zone OU.
Option B: Bring the principal into the zone

If the principal legitimately manages the zone, add it to the zone in pathwarden.toml (members, groups or ous). Then manage it with the same controls as the zone: no use from lower-tier machines, and admin accounts dedicated to this tier.

PowerShell
Import-Module ActiveDirectory

# Show ACEs for the principal on a zone object
$dn = (Get-ADObject -Filter "Name -eq '<Object>'").DistinguishedName
(Get-Acl "AD:$dn").Access | Where-Object { $_.IdentityReference -like "*<Principal>*" } |
    Select-Object IdentityReference, ActiveDirectoryRights, IsInherited

# Remove those ACEs (explicit ones only; fix inherited ones on the parent)
$acl = Get-Acl "AD:$dn"
$acl.Access | Where-Object { $_.IdentityReference -like "*<Principal>*" -and -not $_.IsInherited } |
    ForEach-Object { [void]$acl.RemoveAccessRule($_) }
Set-Acl -Path "AD:$dn" -AclObject $acl

# Local admin rights on a zone computer (run on that computer)
Remove-LocalGroupMember -Group "Administrators" -Member "<Domain>\<Principal>"

Verify

Re-run the Get-Acl command above, or Get-LocalGroupMember -Group "Administrators" on the computer, and confirm the principal is gone. Collect again (or use Retest in the app, or pathwarden retest on the CLI) and confirm the finding ID is gone. Run pathwarden plan --zone "<Zone>" to see what still reaches the zone.

Rollback

Before removing an ACE, export it: (Get-Acl "AD:$dn").Sddl | Out-File acl-backup.txt. Restore it with $acl.SetSecurityDescriptorSddlForm((Get-Content acl-backup.txt)) followed by Set-Acl. Re-add local group members with Add-LocalGroupMember.
